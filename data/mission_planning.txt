SPACE MISSION PLANNING

1. INTRODUCTION

Space mission planning is the process of defining, designing, preparing, and organizing a mission before a spacecraft or satellite is launched.

A space mission may be designed for communication, Earth observation, navigation, scientific research, weather forecasting, planetary exploration, or technology demonstration.

Mission planning involves scientists, aerospace engineers, software engineers, mission analysts, project managers, and safety specialists.

The main purpose of mission planning is to ensure that the mission has clear objectives, a technically feasible design, an appropriate budget, and a realistic schedule.

2. MISSION OBJECTIVES

Every space mission begins with clearly defined objectives.

A mission objective describes what the spacecraft is expected to accomplish.

Examples include:
- Observing weather patterns from orbit
- Providing communication services
- Collecting images of Earth
- Studying the atmosphere
- Exploring the Moon or another planet
- Demonstrating new spacecraft technology

Mission objectives influence the spacecraft design, launch vehicle selection, orbit, instruments, ground systems, and mission duration.

3. FEASIBILITY STUDY

A feasibility study determines whether a proposed mission can realistically be completed.

Engineers and scientists evaluate:
- Available technology
- Required scientific instruments
- Expected mission cost
- Launch opportunities
- Spacecraft mass and power requirements
- Communication requirements
- Technical risks
- Mission schedule

If the mission is not feasible, its objectives or design may be revised.

4. MISSION REQUIREMENTS

Mission requirements describe the conditions that the spacecraft and mission systems must satisfy.

Requirements may include:
- Required imaging resolution
- Expected mission lifetime
- Communication capability
- Electrical power availability
- Temperature limits
- Spacecraft mass limits
- Reliability expectations
- Target orbit

Requirements help engineers verify whether the spacecraft design meets the mission objectives.

5. SPACECRAFT DESIGN

Spacecraft design includes several important subsystems.

The structure provides mechanical support.

The electrical power subsystem generates, stores, and distributes electricity. Many satellites use solar panels and batteries.

The communication subsystem exchanges information with ground stations.

The thermal control subsystem keeps spacecraft components within acceptable temperature ranges.

The attitude determination and control subsystem helps determine and maintain spacecraft orientation.

The onboard computer processes information and coordinates spacecraft functions.

The payload performs the mission's main scientific or operational purpose.

6. ORBIT SELECTION

An orbit is the path followed by a spacecraft around a celestial body.

Low Earth Orbit (LEO) is commonly used for Earth observation, scientific research, and many communication satellite constellations.

Medium Earth Orbit (MEO) is used by several navigation satellite systems.

Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO) allows a satellite in a circular equatorial orbit to appear fixed above one location on Earth's equator.

Polar orbits pass near Earth's poles and can provide coverage of many regions as Earth rotates.

Sun-synchronous orbits are designed so a satellite passes over a location at approximately the same local solar time, making them useful for consistent Earth observation.

Orbit selection depends on the mission objective, coverage requirements, communication needs, and launch capabilities.

7. LAUNCH VEHICLE SELECTION

A launch vehicle transports a spacecraft or payload toward its intended orbit or trajectory.

Selection depends on:
- Payload mass
- Required orbit
- Launch vehicle capability
- Payload dimensions
- Mission schedule
- Reliability considerations
- Available launch services

Examples of launch vehicles include ISRO's PSLV and GSLV families, SpaceX's Falcon 9, and other orbital launch systems.

8. TRAJECTORY PLANNING

A trajectory describes the planned path of a spacecraft during flight.

Mission analysts study how the spacecraft moves under gravitational forces and other influences.

Trajectory planning considers the destination, orbital requirements, fuel limitations, timing, and mission objectives.

For planetary missions, launch windows may be influenced by the relative positions of Earth and the destination.

9. MISSION BUDGET AND SCHEDULE

A mission budget estimates the financial resources required for design, manufacturing, testing, launch, and operations.

A mission schedule defines major milestones such as:
- Concept approval
- Preliminary design review
- Critical design review
- Manufacturing
- System integration
- Environmental testing
- Launch readiness
- Launch
- Mission operations

Delays may occur because of technical problems, weather, manufacturing challenges, or changes in mission requirements.

10. RISK ASSESSMENT

Space missions involve technical and environmental risks.

Possible risks include:
- Launch vehicle failure
- Communication interruptions
- Power system problems
- Thermal issues
- Instrument malfunction
- Software defects
- Orbital debris exposure
- Unexpected environmental conditions

Engineers assess the likelihood and consequences of risks and develop methods to reduce them.

Redundancy, testing, reviews, and fault detection are common risk-reduction approaches.

11. GROUND SEGMENT PLANNING

The ground segment includes the facilities and systems used to communicate with and support a spacecraft.

It may include:
- Ground stations
- Mission control centers
- Data processing facilities
- Communication networks
- Tracking equipment
- Mission planning software

The ground segment supports spacecraft monitoring, communication, and mission data processing.

12. MISSION LIFECYCLE

A typical space mission lifecycle includes:

Concept development
Mission planning
System design
Manufacturing
Integration and testing
Launch preparation
Launch
Deployment and commissioning
Routine operations
End-of-mission activities

The exact lifecycle varies according to the mission.

13. MISSION SUCCESS

Mission success is evaluated against defined objectives and requirements.

A mission may achieve full success, partial success, or fail to meet its primary objectives.

For example, a spacecraft may reach orbit successfully but experience problems with one scientific instrument.

Mission success is therefore broader than launch success alone.

14. END-OF-MISSION PLANNING

Mission planners consider how the spacecraft will be handled when its useful life ends.

Depending on the mission, this may involve:
- Controlled reentry
- Natural orbital decay
- Relocation to a disposal orbit
- Passivation of stored energy sources
- Final data collection

Responsible end-of-mission planning helps reduce long-term space debris risks.

15. SUMMARY

Mission planning transforms a scientific or operational goal into a coordinated space mission.

It includes objective definition, feasibility analysis, spacecraft design, orbit selection, launch planning, risk assessment, ground support, and mission lifecycle management.
